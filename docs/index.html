Ontology specification · Published

HeritageGraph Ontology

HeritageGraph is an event-centric OWL ontology for describing Nepalese living heritage — its tangible objects, sacred places, deities, rituals, festivals, and the communities and institutions (notably the Guthi) that sustain them. Rather than attaching facts directly to objects, the model represents history as a web of events (production, ritual, custody transfer, documentation) so that spatial, temporal, and participatory relationships can be queried and reasoned over. Every assertion can carry its provenance and epistemic standing, allowing competing or multi-vocal sources to coexist. The ontology is generated from a LinkML schema, is an OWL 2 DL vocabulary that is logically consistent under the HermiT reasoner, and is aligned with CIDOC-CRM 7.1.3, CRMinf, CRMsci, and PROV-O, and through a companion module with Linked Art, to maximise interoperability with the wider cultural-heritage Linked Data cloud.

1 Introduction

Nepal's cultural heritage is overwhelmingly living: temples are not merely monuments but active ritual sites, deities are periodically re-consecrated and processioned, and stewardship is mediated by centuries-old socio-religious trusts called Guthi. Documenting this heritage requires more than a catalogue of objects — it requires modelling the events, agents, time-spans, and sources that give the objects their meaning, and doing so in a way that records who said what, on what basis.

Motivation

General cultural-heritage models capture objects and their attributes well, but they struggle with three features that are central to Nepalese living heritage:

  1. Event-centric ritual practice — the same shrine participates in recurring festivals, chariot processions, masked dances, and the selection and retirement of living goddesses (Kumari); these are temporal, sequenced activities, not static properties.
  2. Institutional stewardship — Guthi trusts mediate the relationship between temples, rituals, land, and communities and must be first-class entities.
  3. Contested, multi-source knowledge — construction dates, attributions, and syncretic Hindu–Buddhist identities are frequently disputed across oral histories, archival records, and field surveys, so the model must keep assertion-level provenance and epistemic stance.

State of the art

HeritageGraph builds on, and stays interoperable with, the established standards of the domain: CIDOC-CRM (event-centric documentation), CRMinf (argumentation and belief), CRMsci (observation), and PROV-O (provenance). It complements CIDOC-CRM extensions such as CRMba and CRMsoc by adding the Nepal-specific notions of Guthi-mediated stewardship, ritual activation of sacred space, multi-calendar dating, and multi-source reconciliation.

Goals

The ontology is designed to answer concrete competency questions across four dimensions — structural (architecture, typology, location), ritual/festival (activities, sequences, materials), institutional/syncretic (Guthi, multi-tradition relationships), and living-goddess (Kumari selection, tenure, retirement) — while remaining a clean, reusable OWL vocabulary published under a permanent https://cair-nepal.org/heritagegraph/ identifier.

2 Description

HeritageGraph follows an event-centric modelling pattern inherited from CIDOC-CRM: instead of linking attributes directly to a heritage object, the ontology connects objects, places, agents, and time-spans through events. This makes provenance, sequence, and participation explicit and queryable. The schema contains 84 classes (66 newly minted plus 18 reused from CIDOC-CRM, CRMinf, PROV-O, and SKOS that carry local axioms), 149 object properties, 42 datatype properties, and 7 enumerations published as SKOS concept schemes (51 controlled-vocabulary concepts). It is generated from a LinkML source of truth (HeritageGraph.yaml).

Core building blocks

  • Physical heritage things — PhysicalHeritageThing (a union of ArchitecturalStructure, IconographicObject, and ArchitecturalElement) covers temples (Temple, Stupa, Chaitya, ReligiousStructure), murti/paubha icons (Murti, Paubha), water structures (WaterStructure, DhungeDhara, Pokhari), and rest houses (RestHouse, Pati, Sattal, Dharmashala).
  • Events — heritage change is represented through typed events: production (via CIDOC-CRM crm:E12_Production), Consecration, Enshrinement, custody transfer, RitualEvent (e.g. ChariotFestival, MaskedDance) grouped by Festival, HistoricalEvent, and the living-goddess lifecycle (KumariSelectionEvent, KumariEnthronementEvent, KumariTenure, KumariRetirementEvent, generalised by KumariLifecycleEvent).
  • Actors & institutions — actors and persons (via CIDOC-CRM crm:E39_Actor / crm:E21_Person), CasteGroup, DataCustodian, and the institution central to Nepalese heritage, Guthi (typed by the GuthiType enumeration).
  • Places & time — places and time-spans (via crm:E53_Place / crm:E52_Time-Span) and CalendarSystem support multi-calendar dating (e.g. Bikram Sambat, Nepal Sambat, Gregorian) with explicit DatePrecision.
  • Knowledge & provenance — assertions and beliefs (aligned with CRMinf I2_Belief / Proposition and PROV-O), DataSource (ArchivalRecord, OralHistoryRecording, FieldSurveyDataset), DocumentationActivity, FieldSurveyActivity, and Verification capture who asserted what, from which source, and with what confidence, so competing sources can coexist.
  • Syncretism — SyncreticRelationship links Deity instances that bridge Hindu and Buddhist traditions (ReligiousTradition), typed by the SyncreticType enumeration — as a provenance-qualified interpretive assertion rather than semantic identity (owl:sameAs).

How the pieces relate

A typical pattern looks like this: an ArchitecturalStructure was produced by a production event (crm:E12_Production) that took place at a place (crm:E53_Place), occurred during a time-span (crm:E52_Time-Span), and had a Person or Guthi as participant. A RitualEvent activates the same structure during a Festival, following a canonical sequence. Each fact can be recorded as a source-attributed belief (CRMinf I2_Belief) that was derived from a DataSource and checked by a Verification, so contradictory claims (e.g. two construction dates) coexist with their evidence and epistemic standing.

Interoperability modules

The core vocabulary carries its external alignments inline (SKOS exactMatch / closeMatch / broadMatch, rdfs:subClassOf, and rdfs:subPropertyOf) to CIDOC-CRM, CRMinf, PROV-O, Getty AAT, schema.org, and Wikidata. Companion artefacts:

  • ontology/HeritageGraph.shacl.ttl — SHACL validation shapes for the core vocabulary (75 node shapes).
  • heritagegraph-lux-migration/alignment/heritagegraph-lux-alignment.ttl — a bridge to Yale's LUX / Linked Art (part of the HeritageGraph–LUX migration), with its shapes in ontology/lux/HeritageGraph-lux.shacl.ttl (33 node shapes).
  • examples/kathmandu-mini-abox.ttl — a small worked instance dataset (the demonstrator ABox) that exercises every modelling pattern.

Quality at a glance

The ontology is logically consistent under the HermiT reasoner — 0 unsatisfiable classes, both standalone and when merged with CIDOC-CRM 7.1.3, CRMinf, CRMsci, and PROV-O — and answers all 32/32 competency questions as SPARQL queries over the demonstrator ABox (expected-versus-actual exact match). Its external alignment spans mapping axioms to 11 external vocabularies. See the evaluation suite for reproducible reports.
